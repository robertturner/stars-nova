# Fleet Movement, Scanning, and Cargo

Behavior specification for warp-speed movement, fuel, scanning/detection, and cargo logistics in the 1995-2000 4X game *Stars!*, written for a clean-room reimplementation. All facts below are restated in original wording from public community research — primarily the long-standing *starsfaq.com* "Stars! Advanced and Technical FAQ" ("Guts" reference), the community-maintained *Stars!wiki* at wiki.starsautohost.org (accessed through its Wayback Machine mirrors, since the live site sits behind an active bot-detection challenge), a widely-mirrored GameFAQs strategy guide, and TV Tropes' mechanics summary — plus the official Player's Guide manual (hosted as a public-domain scan on archive.org) for UI-level descriptions. No content was derived from the original binary, any decompilation artifact, or Ghidra output. Where sources are silent, contradictory, or only inferred, this is called out explicitly under "Open Questions" rather than asserted as fact.

## Overview

A *Stars!* fleet moves by being given one or more **waypoints** — destinations with an optional task (colonize, transport cargo, remote-mine, lay mines, scrap, etc.) — and a **warp speed** (1 through 10). Distance covered per year scales with the *square* of the warp factor, so speed has a very steep fuel cost: doubling from warp 5 to warp 10 roughly quadruples the ground covered but the fuel bill grows much faster still, because fuel use scales with both mass and the distance-to-max-distance ratio at that speed. Ships either burn fuel to move (most engines) or, below a per-engine "free" threshold speed, generate a little extra fuel while moving (ramscoop engines). Detection of other empires' fleets and planets is entirely scanner-range-based and split into two tiers — ordinary scanners that only reveal ships/planets while physically in range, and rarer "penetrating" scanners that also reveal a planet's mineral/population/defense details without a ship actually visiting it. Cargo (minerals, colonists, and fuel) is carried in a hull's built-in cargo hold, optionally enlarged with cargo-pod components, and is moved between planets and fleets with per-waypoint load/unload orders, including conditional orders that stop loading/unloading once a target amount is reached. Longer-range logistics can bypass warp travel altogether via player-built Stargates or naturally occurring Wormholes.

## Mechanics

### 1. Warp speed and distance per turn

*Stars!* has ten warp factors. The maximum distance a fleet can cover in a single year at warp factor *N* is `N²` light-years — so warp 4 tops out at 16 ly/year, warp 9 at 81 ly/year, and warp 10 (the fastest speed any ship can ever be ordered to) at 100 ly/year. This relationship is confirmed both by a community minefield-crossing analysis that explicitly measures "100% chance of travelling only 16ly" at warp 4 and notes ships "sometimes travel as far as 81ly at warp 9" (matching 4² and 9² respectively), and by the fan-made *Planets.nu* reimplementation's own help documentation, which states its "Max Distance Per Turn" constant is "equal to warp speed squared." A fleet given a waypoint closer than its maximum range for the chosen speed simply arrives that same year and can act on its task immediately; a fleet given a farther waypoint covers as much of the distance as its ordered speed and available fuel allow and continues from where it stopped the following year.

Each engine component has its own top-line rated speed (e.g., a Fuel Mizer tops out around warp 6; higher-tier engines reach warp 9 or 10 natively), and normal play cannot order a ship faster than its installed engine's rated maximum. **Warp 10 is a special case**: it can be selected for *any* ship regardless of its engine, but unless the engine is one of the small set specifically rated "safe" at warp 10 (e.g., Interspace-10, unlocked via the No Ram Scoop Engines trait, or the endgame Trans-Star 10), every individual ship attempting warp 10 risks outright destruction: a 10% chance per year, rolled independently per ship (not per fleet, and not scaled by how many engines that ship's hull mounts).

A second, unrelated risk applies only to fleets built with the **Cheap Engines** lesser racial trait: any time such a fleet is ordered above warp 6, there is a 10% chance per year that the engines simply fail to engage at all (the fleet does not move that turn, and is not damaged or destroyed — contrast this with the warp-10 destruction risk above, which is a different mechanic keyed to warp 10 specifically rather than "above warp 6"). **Independently re-confirmed by direct observation of the running client** — see `race-traits.md` §3a, which also confirms the exclusive Interspace-10 engine (available to the No Ram Scoop Engines trait) reaches Warp 10 with a full, unconditional exemption from the destruction risk described above, not merely a reduced one.

Separately, scout- and transport-oriented play can push a ship faster than its engine's most fuel-efficient "free" speed simply by accepting a steeper fuel burn, up to the engine's absolute rated maximum — this is informally summarized in community strategy writing as "fuel is speed."

**The per-waypoint Warp Factor control, confirmed by inspection of the exported client.** Each waypoint's ordered speed is stored in the same byte as that waypoint's task-type code (§5 below) — the task type occupies the byte's low nibble, and the warp factor occupies its **high nibble, encoded as `value × 0x10`**. The valid range confirmed in the movement-processing code and independently cross-checked in a fleet-order report/summary routine (which separately formats this same high nibble for display) is exactly the UI's 12-entry range: **0 through 10 as ordinary warp speeds, plus a distinct 12th value (nibble 11, byte value 0xB0) meaning "Use Stargate."** This is a real, separately-stored sentinel, not just a UI label for "attempt a gate automatically": the movement code explicitly branches on whether the stored value is below 0xB0 (ordinary engine movement, using the `N²`-distance formula above) or equal to 0xB0 (routed instead to the Stargate-jump-handling code described in §5's task-dispatcher note below), and a separate overspeed-warning check explicitly excludes the 0xB0 case from its "is this an unusually high warp value" test — confirming 0xB0 is treated as a distinct case throughout, not a large ordinary number. Nibble values 12-15 (0xC0-0xF0) were not observed being produced or checked anywhere and appear unused.

**Warp 0 is a dedicated "hold position" order, not simply the `N²` formula naturally producing zero distance.** The per-turn movement sweep's very first check on a fleet's next waypoint tests whether that waypoint's stored warp nibble is exactly 0 and, if so, skips all of that fleet's movement/arrival processing for the turn outright (the same early-exit branch that also skips fleets on a Transport or Lay-Mine-Field task, both of which are expected to sit still — see §5 below). A fleet can therefore be explicitly ordered to stay put for a turn (e.g. to hold at a waypoint without immediately continuing to the next one), distinct from simply lacking any further waypoints.

### 2. Fuel consumption

**Consumption formula.** A widely-cited community derivation (originally posted to the `rec.games.computer.stars` newsgroup and preserved in the starsfaq.com "Guts" reference) states the baseline rule as: 1 milligram (mg) of fuel moves 200 kilotons (kT) of ship mass 1 light-year, when the relevant engine/speed combination's "Fuel Usage Number" (FUN — a per-engine, per-warp table constant printed in the game's own Guts-of-Engines reference) equals 100. Every other FUN value simply scales this proportionally. In formula form, fuel used per light-year traveled is:

```
FuelPerLY(mg) = (TotalMass_kT / 200) * (FUN / 100) * IFE_multiplier
IFE_multiplier = 0.85 if the race has Improved Fuel Efficiency, else 1.0
TotalFuelForTrip(mg) = FuelPerLY * DistanceTraveled_LY
```

A second, more granular formula is documented by the *Planets.nu* fan reimplementation (a separate, actively-maintained clone of *Stars!*'s ruleset, not the original game) as:

```
FuelUse = TRUNC( FUN * TRUNC(TotalMass_kT / 10) * TRUNC(Distance_LY) / MaxDistancePerTurn / 10000 )
MaxDistancePerTurn = WarpFactor^2
```

This integer-truncating version is algebraically the same relationship rescaled and rounded at different points, and is presented here as a plausible closer match to the original fixed-point engine's actual rounding behavior — but since it comes from a compatible clone rather than the original game, it should be treated as a secondary cross-check rather than a confirmed identical implementation (see Open Questions).

**Confirmed formula, resolved by inspection of the exported client.** The actual per-fleet fuel-cost calculator is `FUN_1050_6510` (`stars.exe.export.c:35638`–`35854`), a previously-uncharacterized segment-11 routine — **not** `FUN_10b0_312a`, which earlier passes had flagged as the likely home of this arithmetic and which is now confirmed (see below) to be unrelated to fuel entirely. `FUN_1050_6510` is called with a fleet pointer, a warp value (or `0xffff` to read the fleet's current waypoint's stored warp nibble, per §1's `>>4` convention), a distance (or `-1` to auto-compute the actual remaining distance to the fleet's next waypoint via the coordinate-delta helper `FUN_1038_2924`), and a mode flag. Concretely, for every occupied design slot in the fleet it:

1. Resolves that design's mounted engine component through the already-documented engine-record accessor `FUN_1008_513e` (`engineIndex * 0x4e + 0x4c38`) and reads the per-warp fuel-table entry at record offset `0x36 + warp*2` (i.e. `+54..+74`, indexed by warp 0-10) — the exact field this project's `extracted-game-data/component-stats.tsv` already tabulates for every engine (`stars.exe.export.c:35712`-`35713`).
2. **Applies Improved Fuel Efficiency directly to that per-warp table value**, not as a separate final multiplier: when a per-race trait-accessor check (`FUN_10e0_226e(<race record>, 0)` — the same generic boolean-trait-accessor family already established elsewhere in this project for other LRTs, here at selector `0`) is true, the value is reduced by exactly `value − value*15/100`, i.e. multiplied by 0.85 (`stars.exe.export.c:35714`-`35723`) — this selector is identified as IFE by its exact behavioral match to IFE's documented −15% fuel effect, not by an independently cross-referenced trait-index table entry.
3. Groups design slots that share an identical (IFE-adjusted) per-warp value into buckets (so multiple ship designs using the same engine/warp combination are combined), and for each bucket accumulates `Σ (designMass × shipCount)` across the bucket's design slots — the mass term reads each design's own cached hull-mass field at design-record-relative offset `0x28` (`stars.exe.export.c:35794`). This is the **universal 16-category record field `+40`/`0x28` = "Mass (kT)"** that `ship-design-and-components.md` §15b establishes applies to every component/hull record, cached into the owning design's own record at the same relative offset — not the *different*, weaker, explicitly-hedged ("not code-execution-confirmed") guess in that same document's §13 that this identical offset holds a cached cargo-capacity value; §13's guess is superseded by its own later §15b/§15e finding that hull cargo capacity actually lives at `+52`, with base armor at `+56`. This cross-file inconsistency in `ship-design-and-components.md` is worth a follow-up correction pass there, but does not weaken the mass identification used here.
4. Computes `fuelForBucket = (FUNvalue_IFEadjusted * distance * bucketMass) / 2000` via a direct 32-bit multiply/divide when the operands stay small, falling back to a percentage/`MulDiv`-style helper (`FUN_1120_0e40`) for larger products to avoid 16-bit-segment overflow (`stars.exe.export.c:35792`-`35810`) — this is a precision/overflow accommodation, not a second formula.
5. Sums `fuelForBucket` across all buckets, and — for the "usage" call (mode `0`) specifically — adds a `+9` rounding term before a final `/10` (`stars.exe.export.c:35827`-`35830`).

**Net result: `FuelUsed = Σ(FUNvalue_IFEadjusted × Distance_LY × TotalMass_kT) / 20000`** (the `/2000` then `/10` compose into `/20000`) — algebraically identical to `(TotalMass_kT / 200) * (FUN / 100) * IFE_multiplier * Distance_LY`, i.e. **this confirms the starsfaq.com-derivation shape (§2's first formula block above), not the Planets.nu-derivation shape**: nowhere in this divisor chain does `MaxDistancePerTurn` (`WarpFactor²`) appear as a divisor — the warp-squared distance cap is a wholly separate computation performed upstream, in the caller, to decide how far the fleet travels this turn in the first place (see below), not folded into the per-light-year fuel-rate formula itself the way Planets.nu's documented formula folds it in. This also resolves the version-dependence concern (Open Questions, "Fuel-generation table's patch-version dependence"): a clean-room implementation should simply use this exact binary's own `component-stats.tsv`-recovered per-warp values (confirmed to be exactly what this code reads), sidestepping the question of which wiki/starsfaq snapshot's table matches this particular build.

**Caller-side confirmation of fuel deduction and the fuel-exhaustion rule, resolved by inspection of the exported client.** `FUN_10b0_1f8c` (already documented above as the Stargate/waypoint-task caller) calls `FUN_1050_6510` multiple times per fleet, per turn, around the same `stars.exe.export.c:73400`-`73650` region already cited for the §1 warp² distance clamp:

- First, once with mode `1` (a fixed reference distance of 1000) and once with mode `0` and the auto-computed *full* remaining distance to the next waypoint (`stars.exe.export.c:73422`-`73424`), comparing the combined result against the fleet's currently-stored fuel quantity (a 64-bit field at fleet-record-relative offset `0x2e`/`0x2f`) to set a "can this fleet afford its whole remaining trip" flag (`local_58`, `stars.exe.export.c:73428`-`73434`).
- **If the fleet has enough fuel for the full remaining distance, the ordinary `N²`-warp distance cap (§1) is applied as normal** (`stars.exe.export.c:73436`-`73446`) and the fleet travels as far as its ordered warp speed allows this turn. **If it does not, that clamp is skipped**, leaving the smaller, fuel-derived distance value already computed in place — i.e. **an underfueled fleet's travel this turn is capped to the distance its remaining fuel can actually pay for, rather than completing the full ordered-speed hop and only afterward clamping fuel to zero.** This resolves the "silently completes a partial hop vs. downgrades to an affordable speed" open question in favor of the partial-hop reading, though the exact sub-formula that converts the mode-`1` reference result into that capped distance value was not traced arithmetic-term-by-term, so this is offered as well-evidenced rather than microscopically verified.
- Once the turn's actual travel distance is finalized, `FUN_1050_6510` is called again with mode `0` and that actual distance (`stars.exe.export.c:73630`), and its result is **subtracted directly from the fleet's stored fuel field**, clamped to zero rather than allowed to go negative (`stars.exe.export.c:73639`-`73647`) — confirming fuel is deducted turn-by-turn by direct subtraction of a freshly-recomputed cost, not by consulting a precomputed range.

**`FUN_10b0_312a` itself is confirmed to have no role in fuel consumption at all.** A full line-by-line read of its ordinary-movement (non-Stargate) call path (`stars.exe.export.c:74080`-`74701`) — including the design-mass-accumulation-shaped loop at `74297`-`74316` and `74404`-`74476` that an earlier pass had flagged as a promising fuel-formula candidate — shows that loop instead computes a **total fleet ship-count** (`local_2e`, used to gate a minefield-hit adjustment term) and a **per-design mass/armor-quantization figure** consumed by mine-hit ship-loss arithmetic (the `/500` division at `stars.exe.export.c:74449`-`74451` matches this project's already-confirmed 1/500 armor-quantization scheme from `combat-resolution.md`, not a fuel divisor). The function's warp-nibble-indexed table lookups at `74376`-`74382` index into fixed mine-damage-constant tables by a minefield-tier/type bucket selector, not by the fleet's ordered warp speed. Every code path in this function's non-Stargate call is minefield-transit hit-chance, damage, and ship-loss arithmetic, exactly as the earlier pass's "dominated by minefield-transit arithmetic" characterization already concluded — this pass extends that from a hedge to a confirmed negative result specifically for the fuel question, now that the real fuel calculator (`FUN_1050_6510`) has been found elsewhere.

**Fuel generation (ramscoops).** Below its rated "free" warp threshold, a ramscoop-type engine produces fuel instead of consuming it, and every engine (ramscoop or not) produces a small amount of fuel at warp 1 specifically. Per-engine fuel generated over a distance `D` (at full-throttle travel for that speed) follows a step pattern relative to how far below the engine's free-travel speed it is running:

```
F = 0   -- running above the engine's free-travel warp (fuel is being consumed, not generated)
F = D   -- running exactly at the engine's free-travel warp
F = 3D  -- running 1 warp factor below the free-travel warp
F = 6D  -- running 2 warp factors below the free-travel warp
F = 10D -- running 3+ warp factors below the free-travel warp
```

This is per engine, not per ship — a hull with 2, 3, or 4 engines generates (or burns) proportionally more fuel. The Stars!wiki's own generation table (reproduced below, in mg, current as of the "JRC4" game patch — the wiki explicitly warns older patches had different, buggier values) shows every conventional (non-scoop) engine generating exactly 1 mg at warp 1, which is the mechanical basis for treating warp 1 as an always-available, self-sustaining crawl speed regardless of remaining fuel:

**Improved Fuel Efficiency (IFE)** lesser racial trait reduces all fuel consumption by 15% across every engine, and unlocks the Fuel Mizer, Trans-Galactic Mizer Scoop, and Galaxy Scoop engines.

**Radiation hazard of low-tier ramscoops.** The cheapest ramscoop, the Radiating Hydro-Ram Scoop, is documented as gradually harming colonists riding along in the same fleet (unless the race's ideal radiation setting is very high), which in practice restricts that engine to mineral-only hauling rather than mixed colonist/cargo runs.

**Running out of fuel.** Fleet fuel is depleted during the normal movement phase of turn processing, alongside minefield hits, stargate jumps, and wormhole transits. Because every known engine generates a small positive amount of fuel at warp 1 (see table above), a fleet that exhausts its fuel reserve is not permanently stranded — warp 1 remains available indefinitely as a self-sustaining minimum speed. **The precise turn-by-turn behavior when a fleet is ordered to a speed its remaining fuel cannot fully pay for is now resolved** — see the "Caller-side confirmation of fuel deduction and the fuel-exhaustion rule" note above: the fleet's travel distance for the turn is capped to whatever its remaining fuel can afford (a silent partial hop), rather than completing the full ordered-speed jump and only then clamping fuel to zero.

### 3. Scanning and detection

*Stars!* draws a hard line between two scanner tiers:

- **Standard (non-penetrating) scanners** only reveal a fleet or planet's presence and basic identity while a scan-equipped ship or planet is within range of it; they cannot read a distant, un-visited planet's mineral concentration, population, or defenses.
- **Penetrating scanners** additionally reveal a planet's full attributes (and any ships/starbase in orbit there) from range, without ever having to physically visit or orbit it — the effective coverage area is shown as a distinct overlay in the game's Scanner Pane. Penetrating scanner technology first becomes available at Electronics tech level 10; a homeworld's very first ordinary planetary scanner is meaningfully weaker (roughly 150 ly at Electronics tech 3).

**No Advanced Scanners (NAS)** is a lesser racial trait that trades away all penetrating-scanner technology in exchange for doubling the range of every conventional scanner the race builds (e.g., a race's Electronics-tech-3 homeworld scanner reaches 300 ly instead of 150 ly under NAS; the top-tier "Snooper 620X" scanner reaches roughly double its listed range under NAS as well). Some racial abilities keep a narrow slice of penetrating capability even under NAS — e.g., a Jack of All Trades race's built-in Scout/Frigate/Destroyer scanner, a Packet Physics race's mineral-packet scanner, or a Super-Stealth race's Chameleon/Robber Baron components — because those are treated as inherent racial abilities rather than the generic "planet penetrating scanner" technology NAS disables.

**Combining multiple scanners on one design.** When a single ship or starbase design carries more than one scanner component, its effective range is not a simple maximum or sum — it is computed as the fourth root of the sum of each individual scanner's range raised to the fourth power:

```
CombinedRange = ( Σ scanner_i_range^4 ) ^ (1/4)
```

For example, a design carrying two 100-ly scanners and one 60-ly scanner has a combined range of `(100^4 + 100^4 + 60^4)^(1/4) ≈ 120` ly — noticeably more than any single component alone, but far short of a simple sum. The same combination rule is described as applying to penetrating scanners as well.

**Cloaking vs. scanning.** A cloak reduces the effective range at which a scanner can detect the cloaked fleet by the cloak's percentage — e.g., an 80%-cloaked fleet is only detectable once an observer is within 20% of that scanner's normal rated range (so a 150-ly scanner would only spot it inside 30 ly). Because planetary scanners are stationary, an opponent who knows (or conservatively estimates) their range can plan routes that stay just outside it; mobile scout ships are correspondingly more effective at catching cloaked fleets specifically because their position is not predictable turn to turn. Minefields belonging to a Space Demolition race act as an additional, distinct detection layer against cloaked fleets that enter them: the chance of detecting a cloaked fleet inside such a minefield in a given year is `100% − cloak%` (e.g., an 80%-cloaked fleet is spotted 20% of the time per year it sits in the minefield), independent of ordinary scanner range.

**Packet Physics** races get a race-specific scanning shortcut: every mineral packet they fling carries a built-in penetrating scanner whose range equals the square of the packet's own travel warp speed, letting them "scan ahead" of their own mineral shipments.

**Scanning formula, verified against the exported client.** The turn-generation engine's actual detection test (run once per turn for every fleet-vs-fleet and planet-vs-fleet pair) is `distance² <= scanRange²`, with the scanning side's effective range scaled per-dimension by `(100 - cloak%) / 100` before squaring — consistent with, and a more precise restatement of, this document's "effective range reduced by cloak%" claim above. Separate range lookups exist for a fleet's own scan range versus a planet's, though both use the same distance-squared test. **Minefield detection** is confirmed to use a distinct formula from ordinary scanning: `(warningFieldValue + 4)`, squared, as a flat detection radius — independent of the cloak-based scanner test. **Wormhole (and similar unusual-object) detection** is confirmed to be genuinely probabilistic rather than a deterministic range check: a flat radius test is combined with a random roll (0-99) against the observed object's cloak percentage, meaning a wormhole or similar object can go undetected even at close range on any given turn. This document's existing multi-scanner-combination (fourth-root) formula and per-engine detection-range table were not independently checked against the executable.

**Starbase cloaking in the planet sweeps, confirmed by inspection of the exported client.** A starbase design carries its own cloak percentage, computed once a turn in turn-generation step 36 (`turn-generation-engine.md` §1 step 36): 20% built in for a race with Improved Starbases, 75% for Super Stealth, plus whatever its installed components add, capped at 98%. The stored figure is `(100 - cloak%)²`. When a scanner's range already reaches a planet that has an owned starbase, the planet is reported at full detail, starbase included, only if the squared distance is at most that figure times the squared range divided by 10,000, i.e. within range x (100 - cloak%)/100; otherwise the planet is seen but its starbase stays hidden. Ships, planetary scanners and Packet Physics mass-packet scanners all apply this same test (`FUN_1070_5f00`, `FUN_1070_64fc`, `FUN_1070_6d7c`). Unlike a cloaked fleet, a starbase is not subject to the observer's Tachyon Detector counter-cloak multiplier.

### 4. Cargo capacity and cargo transfer

Every hull has a fixed base cargo capacity in kilotons (kT), shared by minerals, colonists, and (on most hulls) fuel is tracked as a *separate* pool from cargo rather than counting against it. Cargo-pod components can be mounted in eligible slots to add further capacity. Representative base cargo capacities documented for stock hulls:

| Hull | Base cargo (kT) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Small Freighter | 70 | |
| Medium Freighter | 210 | requires Construction tech 3 |
| Large Freighter | 1200 | requires Construction tech 8 |
| Super Freighter | 3000 | requires Construction tech 13; Inner Strength race only |
| Privateer | 250 | requires Construction tech 4 |
| Rogue | 500 | requires Construction tech 8; Super-Stealth race only |
| Galleon | 1000 | requires Construction tech 11 |
| Meta Morph | 300 | requires Construction tech 10; Hyper-Expansion race only |
| Mini-Colony Ship | 10 | Hyper-Expansion race only |

Fuel-tanker hulls are a separate special case: the Fuel Transport (750 mg fuel capacity) and Super-Fuel Xport (2250 mg) hulls actively manufacture roughly 200 mg of fuel per year while deployed and additionally raise the healing rate of other ships sharing their fleet.

**Loading and unloading.** Cargo movement is configured per-waypoint as a task, with options including loading/unloading all of a mineral type, loading/unloading a fixed amount, and a conditional form — informally "Set Waypoint to: `<amount>`" — that only loads (or only unloads) as much as needed to bring the source (or destination)'s on-hand quantity up to (or down to) a target level, rather than blindly moving everything available. This conditional form is the documented way to run a fully automated repeat-route between two planets (e.g., a supply run and a mineral-balancing run) without the ship ever over-draining either endpoint. Colonist cargo can also be loaded/unloaded the same way as minerals; Inner Strength race colonists additionally continue to reproduce at a reduced rate while riding in a fleet's cargo hold, spilling any surplus onto a planet the fleet is orbiting.

**Confirmed formula, verified against the exported client.** The conditional "Set Waypoint to: `<amount>`" task is resolved once per turn by computing, across up to 4 cargo slots (consistent with Ironium/Boranium/Germanium/Colonists), a percent-of-target-reached value `pct = ((amountSoFar + 1) × 100) / target - 1`, then acting on whichever slot is **least complete** (the most limiting resource) that round — the fleet keeps transferring in bounded chunks until a transfer comes in under the computed cap, rather than moving everything in one step. Separately, a per-race lookup selects a colonist/fuel transfer-chunk divisor of **100 normally, or 25 for some races** — a roughly 4× faster per-round colonist/fuel transfer rate, now resolved to a real (if branchless) in-code check rather than raw save data — see below.

**The "100 vs. 25" divisor, resolved this session — it is a genuine code branch, not per-race save data as previously guessed.** The earlier conclusion that this value must be read directly out of per-race save data was a reasonable inference from a negative literal-value search, but the actual computation was simply missed by that search because it's written as a branchless arithmetic idiom rather than an `if`/`else` with both constants visible together. At `stars.exe.export.c:76837`-`76839`, inside `FUN_10b0_3f3a`'s Transport handler:

```
iVar9 = FUN_10e0_226e(<owning race's trait-flag record>, 6);
uVar4 = (-(uint)(iVar9 == 0) & 0x4b) + 0x19;   // trait clear: 0x4b(75)+0x19(25) = 100; trait set: 0+0x19 = 25
```

`FUN_10e0_226e` is this project's already-established generic per-race **boolean** trait-flag accessor (the same family used, e.g., at `stars.exe.export.c:35680` with selector `0` to gate this document's own newly-confirmed Improved Fuel Efficiency fuel-formula adjustment above, and at selector `0xc` for the ship-design cost-estimator gate `ship-design-and-components.md` §8 already documents) — here called with **selector `6`**. `-(cond) & 0x4b`, added to `0x19`, is a standard branchless "value ? A : B" pattern: when the trait bit is clear the expression reduces to `0x4b + 0x19 = 0x64 = 100`; when set, it reduces to `0 + 0x19 = 25`. This is why a search for a literal `0x19` sitting next to a literal `0x64` never found it — the two constants that actually sum to 100 are `0x4b` (75) and `0x19` (25), and `0x64` itself never appears as a literal anywhere in this computation.

**Selector `6` is now identified: Mineral Alchemy (MA), resolved this session by cross-referencing every other call site of the identical selector.** A whole-executable search for `FUN_10e0_226e(...,6)` finds exactly three call sites, all sharing the identical branchless 100-vs-25 idiom: this one (`stars.exe.export.c:76837`), one inside `FUN_10d0_221a` — this project's already-documented, `production-queue.md` §5-§7-confirmed generic per-item-type cost calculator — at `stars.exe.export.c:87847`-`87848`, and one inside AI auto-build code (`FUN_1090_420c`, `stars.exe.export.c:64355`-`64357`). The second site settles the question directly: it sits in `FUN_10d0_221a`'s joint `case 3: case 0xb:` branch, which `production-queue.md` §7 independently identifies (via the game's own help text) as the Mineral Alchemy production-queue item — "Each unit of mineral alchemy will turn a mere 100 of your resources (25 if you have the Mineral Alchemy trait) into 1 kT of each of the three minerals." The exact same selector, the exact same 100-or-25 output, and the exact same "trait clear → 100, trait set → 25" polarity make this an unambiguous match: **selector `6` of `FUN_10e0_226e` is Mineral Alchemy.** The third site independently corroborates this from yet another angle: `FUN_1090_420c` (part of the AI's auto-build advisory pass documented in `ai-opponent-behavior.md`) reuses the identical selector-6/100-25 idiom, scaled by a population-derived figure, to size an auto-queued production item that it then queues via item-type code `0xb` (`stars.exe.export.c:74373`/`74376`, `FUN_1090_2736(0xb, ...)`) — the same `0xb` item-type code `FUN_10d0_221a`'s `case 3: case 0xb:` prices as Mineral Alchemy. Three independent call sites, two of them cross-confirming each other through the identical item-type code, leave no real ambiguity.

This resolves this document's own open question about which named race/trait selector `6` represents, and reveals a previously-unnoticed **cross-mechanic coupling**: the same boolean flag that makes Mineral Alchemy cost 25 resources per kT (instead of 100) also makes a fleet's automated colonist/fuel cargo transfers close the remaining gap roughly 4x faster per round. There is no obvious in-universe reason these two effects should share one flag rather than each having its own dedicated selector — but the code is unambiguous on this point, and this project has already documented other cases of one boolean flag being reused for behaviorally unrelated purposes (see the cargo-pod/fuel-tank bonus calculator's category reuse above, and the AI production code's reuse of this exact idiom for a third purpose). Read literally, a race with the Mineral Alchemy lesser racial trait also gets faster automated Transport-task cargo transfers as an (likely unintentional, or at least undocumented in any public source) side effect. This project has so far pinned down three selectors of this generic accessor with confidence: `0` for Improved Fuel Efficiency, `6` for Mineral Alchemy (this pass), and `0xc` for the ship-design cost-estimator/Bleeding Edge Technology-linked gate documented in `ship-design-and-components.md` §8. The per-slot cargo/fuel delta helper the Transport handler calls into (`FUN_1050_3cee`, `stars.exe.export.c:33394`) remains, as previously found, a generic bounded add/subtract accessor uninvolved in selecting this divisor.

**Per-hull pod-slot limits are now directly readable from each hull's own slot array, also recovered in `component-stats.tsv`'s category `0x4000` table.** Each hull record lists its slots as a sequence of allowed-category groups (e.g. Privateer: `Engx1 Shld+Armx2 Scan+Elec+Mechx1 Scan+Shld+Arm+Beam+Torp+MLay+Elec+Mechx1 Scan+Shld+Arm+Beam+Torp+MLay+Elec+Mechx1`). Every slot whose allowed-category group includes "Mech" (category `0x1000`) can mount *either* a cargo pod *or* a fuel tank *or* any other Mechanical-category item (Colonization Module, Overthruster, Beam Deflector, etc.) — the game does not reserve separate "cargo-pod-only" and "fuel-pod-only" slot types; it is one shared category gate per slot, matching this document's existing "eligible slots" framing in the Overview. Representative Mech-capable slot counts read directly from the table: **Small/Medium Freighter — 1** Mech-capable slot (shared "Scan+Elec+Mech" group); **Large Freighter — 2**; **Privateer — 3** (1 "Scan+Elec+Mech" plus 2 fully-generic "everything" slots that also include Mech); **Rogue — 6** across four Mech-including slot groups; **Galleon** and other late-game hulls carry proportionally more. Cross-checking the same table's `cargo`/`fuel` base columns against this document's existing hull table (§4) confirms an exact match for every hull previously listed (Small Freighter 70/130, Medium Freighter 210/450, Large Freighter 1200/2600, Privateer 250/650, Rogue 500/2250), which corroborates that `component-stats.tsv`'s hull records are the same data this document's base-cargo-capacity table already draws on, not a separate or inconsistent source.

**Fleet lifecycle, verified against the exported client.** A fleet is a fixed 124-byte record; fleet IDs are capped at **512 per empire** (an 8-bit fleet-number sub-field, plus a design-slot sub-field capped at the well-known 16-designs-per-race limit). Creating a fleet finds the smallest unused ID for that owner; deleting one frees its cargo/order sub-blocks and removes it from the owner's live-fleet list. Fleets sharing an exact same-turn location are linked into a same-location "stack," rebuilt once per turn/redraw — this is the concrete data structure behind the game's multi-fleet-per-tile display, not previously documented in this specification. **Split Fleet** clones off single-ship fleets per design slot from a source fleet; **Merge Fleets** sums ship counts, cargo, and mass into the primary fleet and deletes the absorbed fleets.

**Merge Fleets mechanics, verified against the exported client.** The merge dialog lists every fleet at the same location, pre-selecting the fleet the merge was invoked from (and, when exactly two fleets are present, both). For each of up to 16 ship-design slots being merged: designs already at 100% fuel merge with no penalty; designs below 100% fuel undergo a **probabilistic fuel-synchronization pass** — a random roll (weighted by remaining fuel fraction and a per-design chance) can strand some ships behind rather than merging them, producing one of five graduated warning messages depending on how many ships were left behind. Designs with genuinely incompatible fuel or cargo capacities abort the merge entirely rather than partially merging. After a successful merge, per-ship special-ability stats (scanning, cloaking, and similar) are recombined: 6 scanner-type fields take the **maximum** value across all merged ships, while a separate 12-slot ability tally **sums** contributions, capped at **25**.

### 5. Waypoints and multi-turn movement orders

A fleet's full route is an ordered list of waypoints, each with its own optional task; the fleet moves toward its next unresolved waypoint every turn at its ordered warp speed and executes that waypoint's task once it actually arrives, then proceeds to the following waypoint the next turn. A **Repeat Orders** flag, valid when the final waypoint matches the starting point, causes the whole waypoint list to restart automatically once completed, which combined with conditional load/unload amounts (see above) is the documented way to build a "set once, forget it" supply route. Distances snap to a fixed one-light-year grid for waypoint placement (diagonal legs are a decimal amount slightly over one light-year per grid step). A route leg that exactly repeats an already-traveled leg (e.g., an out-and-back path) is drawn differently (white) from other legs in the UI.

**No dynamic interception of a moving fleet, best-supported reading from inspection of the exported client.** Each waypoint record is a fixed 18-byte entry: a target-planet-index field (or a "deep space" sentinel), an x/y coordinate pair, a task-type nibble, and a small block of task-specific parameters (matching the layout independently confirmed via the fleet-creation routine's own initial-waypoint setup, which copies the fleet's spawn planet's position — read from the same planet-position table the map painter uses — directly into this same x/y field once, at creation time). The per-turn movement/task-processing loop (the master turn-generation routine's fleet sweep) reads this stored x/y directly when computing how far the fleet can travel and whether it has arrived; no code path in that loop was found that re-resolves the waypoint's coordinates from a live fleet position before using them. This is consistent with — and reinforced by — the already-documented **Merge With Fleet** queued task (§ above): its target fleet is looked up by ID only "at the same map position" once the merging fleet has already arrived at its stored destination coordinates, i.e. a fleet-ID reference in the data model is used for validating what's present *after* arrival, not for steering *toward* that fleet's current position while it's still moving. Taken together, this supports reading Stars! as having **no automatic pursuit/interception mechanic**: setting a course toward another fleet (however the UI exposes that) most plausibly just captures that fleet's position at the moment the order is given, the same as clicking empty space, and catching a moving target is left entirely to the player re-issuing orders turn by turn as it moves. This is offered as the best-supported conclusion from the code inspected, not a certainty — the UI-side waypoint-placement code (as opposed to the turn-generation-side code that consumes the resulting waypoint) was not itself traced in this pass, so a live-play check (plotting a course onto a moving enemy fleet and watching whether the destination marker updates on subsequent turns) would be the decisive confirmation.

**Partially traced this session — a genuine UI-side (not turn-generation-side) consumer of the same static waypoint layout was located, though the specific mouse-click "place a new waypoint" write path was not.** `FUN_1038_26ac` (`stars.exe.export.c:19100`-`19240`-ish) is a route/waypoint-list feasibility routine that walks a fleet's stored waypoint array leg by leg (indexing consecutive 18-byte records via the confirmed `+0x12` stride) and, for each leg, calls the same coordinate-delta helper (`FUN_1038_2924`) this document's turn-generation-side finding already relies on, to test whether the leg's distance is coverable at the fleet's warp speed (falling back to a Stargate-capability check, `FUN_1038_4ae4`, when the plain-engine test fails) before setting the report-row's text color — this is, concretely, the "sets a report-row's text color (a 'feasible' vs. 'out of range' red/gray distinction)... for a route/waypoint list cell" function `client-ui-dialog-catalog.md`'s segment-8 sampling pass already independently found and attributed to the UI's route/waypoint list (see that document's "genuine GDI-touching functions" note). Because this function reads each waypoint's x/y directly out of the stored array — the same static-field read pattern already confirmed for the turn-generation side — and never re-resolves a leg's endpoint from a live fleet position, it extends (without yet completing) this section's "no live re-resolution anywhere" claim to a second, genuinely UI-side code path (a route-list display/feasibility check that runs interactively as the player edits orders, not just once per turn). **What this does not yet confirm:** the actual write path invoked when a player clicks the map to add or move a waypoint — the routine that decides what x/y value gets stored into a new waypoint record in the first place — was searched for but not located this pass (a blind search for the 18-byte-record insert/shift pattern in the client's lower-numbered, UI-associated segments turned up dozens of unrelated `* 0x12` stride uses with no anchor narrow enough to isolate it in the time available). The live-play check this document already proposes as the decisive confirmation remains the more direct way to close this out; this pass's contribution is one additional, code-confirmed UI-side data point consistent with the existing conclusion, not a completion of it.

**Stargates** let a fleet skip warp travel across arbitrary distance in a single turn, provided both the origin and destination planets have a stargate, the fleet carries only fuel (see Cargo, above), and the ship's mass and the distance requested both fall within the *sending* stargate's rated limits (mass checks additionally require the *receiving* gate's rating too). A gate can be pushed up to 5x over its rated distance or mass limit and still sometimes succeed — this "overgating" always damages the ship, and beyond 100% cumulative damage the ship does not survive the jump. Documented overgating damage/loss formulas:

```
RangeDamagePercent = 100% * (Distance - MaxRange) / (4 * MaxRange)
MassDamagePercent  = 100% * (1 - ((5*MaxSendMass - Mass)/(4*MaxSendMass)) * ((5*MaxReceiveMass - Mass)/(4*MaxReceiveMass)))
CombinedDamagePercent = MassDamagePercent + (100% - MassDamagePercent) * RangeDamagePercent

# Community-fitted approximation for the chance a mass-overgated ship vanishes outright (non-IT races):
P_vanish(%) = (100 - A) * (5*MaxMass - Mass)^2 / (4*MaxMass)^2 + A     -- fitted constant A ≈ 68
# For distance overgating, vanish chance is roughly RangeDamagePercent / 3 as a rule of thumb.
```

Interstellar Traveler races are documented as having a reduced (but not quantified) chance of losing overgated ships to the void, while suffering the same damage percentages as anyone else. The client's own description text for this trait independently confirms the existence and framing of this reduced risk (still without a numeric figure) — see `race-traits.md` §3a.

**Wormholes** are naturally occurring, free, uncapped-mass shortcuts between two points in deep space (they cannot form too close to a planet's gravity well). A fleet given a wormhole as a waypoint enters and exits the same year it reaches the opening. Wormholes are individually cloaked against ordinary detection by 75% until a player has discovered them at least once, and each end independently drifts a little every year, with an overall stability rating (from "Rock Solid," lasting 30+ years in one area, down to "Very Unstable," relocating within about 5 years) describing how long a given wormhole persists before disappearing or relocating.

**Wormhole placement and stability, confirmed by inspection of the exported client.** Candidate wormhole (and similar "special object") positions are validated by a dedicated placement-scoring routine before being accepted: a candidate is rejected outright for landing outside galaxy bounds, exactly on an existing planet, fleet, or other special object, and otherwise scored against every planet and every same-type special object in the galaxy using four squared-distance tiers (very close / close / medium / far), producing a bitmask a caller can use to keep re-rolling until a sufficiently isolated position is found — the same rejection-sampling shape already documented for ordinary star placement in `new-game-setup.md` §3, reused here for special-object spawning. Separately, a stability-rating index is derived from a packed per-object data field and clamped to **exactly 7 tiers (0-6)**, consistent with, and numerically confirming, the "Rock Solid" through "Very Unstable" rating scale described above. A same-location, opposite-race fleet check against a wormhole-type object sums three of the fleet's stored fields — **now confirmed to be specifically the fleet's three mineral cargo pools (Ironium + Boranium + Germanium), not a generic mass/cargo total** — against a flat threshold of **5000**: below the threshold, a single fixed notification message is posted to the fleet's owner (through the report/message-record store described below, gated on a per-fleet "already notified" flag).

**Heavy-fleet transit arithmetic, resolved by inspection of the exported client.** *(Correction from the turn-order reconciliation: the routine described in this note and the next, `FUN_1118_0784`, is the Mystery Trader encounter routine, not a wormhole check. It scans special-object entries of category `0x60`, which `turn-generation-engine.md` §10 identifies as the Trader, and its messages are the Trader's (264-266, 270, 280). Read "wormhole" as "Mystery Trader" in this note; see `turn-generation-engine.md` §9 and §1 step 23c. The arithmetic below is unaffected.)* At or above the 5000-mineral threshold, the routine first checks a per-wormhole, per-race "already processed this turn" bitmask; if already set for this race, a distinct message is posted and nothing further happens. Otherwise it reads the transiting race's 6-value per-gas environmental-tolerance array (the same array used elsewhere for habitat/terraforming checks) and compares each value in turn against a threshold of 26 or 10 (selected by an unrelated per-race trait bit) until one falls short. **If all six values clear the threshold** (a broadly gas-tolerant race), the branch instead rolls a flat 1-in-5 chance: on success the fleet is relocated to a new random galaxy position (through the same relocation routine used for other forced-repositioning effects) with a distinct "wormhole malfunction" message; on failure, nothing happens — the fleet passes through unaffected either way (never destroyed or damaged by this check). **For every other (ordinary) race**, an integer tier is computed as `tier = min(10, 6 + floor((totalMinerals - 5000) / 1200))` — i.e., the tier starts at 6 exactly at the threshold and increases by 1 for every further 1200 kT of mineral cargo aboard, capped at 10 — then adjusted downward by the *sum* of that same race's 6 tolerance values: a sum of 108+ forces the tier to a flat 1; 96-107 forces it to 2; 84-95 subtracts 3; 72-83 or 60-71 subtracts 1; 59 or below leaves the tier unmodified. One of two flavor-only messages (carrying no numeric payload) is posted, chosen by a per-race relations-bitmask flag. **The resulting tier (1-10) is not itself a pass/fail outcome — it is used directly as the iteration count for a stochastic per-gas habitat-tolerance-drift process**, which is the *same* mechanic already documented, without its trigger identified, in `turn-generation-engine.md` §9 ("Fleet-linked habitat-tolerance drift"): each iteration rolls a ~1-in-4 gate, then either a ~1-in-6 sub-check against a randomly chosen gas or (on failure/fallback) picks the race's currently-weakest tolerance gas, and — provided that gas isn't already at the threshold — nudges its tolerance value by exactly ±1, recording the change into a per-race running-statistics table. This resolves both previously-open questions together: the wormhole "mass check" never redirects, damages, or destroys the fleet itself in the ordinary case; instead, heavier mineral cargo (in 1200 kT steps above 5000, capped at 10) causes that race's environment-tolerance genome to drift *more times* this turn, while a race whose tolerances are already broad drifts *less* (or is diverted into the separate 20%-relocation branch above). It also identifies turn-generation-engine.md §9's "gated to run only when at least one race has a specific primary racial trait" condition as this same "all six tolerance bytes already broad" state — though which named PRT this corresponds to still could not be determined, since no string/label data survives in the analyzed material.

**Traced further this session — the "26 or 10" threshold selector is not a PRT-index comparison at all, which revises the premise of the open question above.** Reading `FUN_1118_0784` (segment 36, `stars.exe.export.c:115255`–`115814`) directly at the point where the threshold is chosen (`stars.exe.export.c:115354`) shows: `local_1a = (-(uint)((flagByte & 2) == 0) & 0x10) + 10;` — i.e. the threshold is 10 when a specific bit (`0x2`) of a per-race flag byte is set, or 26 when it is clear. That flag byte is read from a per-race table (`DAT_1128_5a16`, indexed by race number × `0xc0`) that is *not* the PRT-index accessor used everywhere else in this project (`FUN_10e0_222c`/`FUN_10e0_226e` reading a race record's offset `0x3e`, per `ship-design-and-components.md` §5's established convention) — no call to that accessor, or any other read of the confirmed PRT-index field, appears anywhere in this threshold-selection code path. The subsequent "all six clear" check (`stars.exe.export.c:115356`–`115361`, `iStack_4==6`) is then a pure data comparison of the *transiting race's own six tolerance byte values* (which are player-set/randomized at race creation, like any other habitability tolerance) against whichever threshold was selected — not a property fixed by PRT at all. **This means the premise carried in `turn-generation-engine.md` §9 and repeated above — that this branch is "gated to run only when at least one race has a specific primary racial trait" — does not hold up under direct inspection of the threshold-selection code itself:** any race of any PRT can enter the "all six clear" branch if its own rolled tolerance values happen to be wide enough (doubly so if it also carries the unidentified flag bit that lowers the required threshold from 26 to 10, which makes qualifying easier, not tied to a single named trait either). No named PRT answers "which PRT has broad gas tolerance" because the mechanic is not keyed to a PRT in the first place; the open item going forward is instead identifying what the `DAT_1128_5a16` bit-`0x2` per-race flag represents (a distinct, narrower question this pass did not resolve, since `DAT_1128_5a16` is reused for several unrelated per-race scratch purposes elsewhere in segment 23 — see the duplicate-name-check use of the same table at `stars.exe.export.c:71828` — and this pass could not confirm this specific bit's meaning from a single call site). `turn-generation-engine.md` §9 should be revised to drop the "specific primary racial trait" framing in favor of "a per-race tolerance-data comparison, modulated by an unidentified per-race flag bit," pending that further trace.

**Further traced this session — `DAT_1128_5a16` bit `0x2`'s identity, best-supported reading: a generic per-race scratch/state flag, not a race-creation trait selection at all.** A whole-executable enumeration of every read/write of `DAT_1128_5a16` (30 call sites) shows the byte sits at a fixed offset (`+0x54`) inside the *same* 192-byte per-race record already established elsewhere in this project (base `DAT_1128_59c2`, `raceIndex * 0xc0` stride — the record `FUN_10e0_226e`/`FUN_10e0_222c` also index into, just at a different byte offset, `+0x4e`, for the LRT/PRT-facing trait accessor), but it is read through *direct* byte/bitwise access rather than through that accessor family, and its bits do double, unrelated duty depending on call site: bit `0x1` gates roughly a dozen "is this race slot still active/valid" checks scattered across segments 8, 23, and others (e.g. `stars.exe.export.c:8040`, `20107`, `25306`, `44609`, `76042`, `79326`); No named LRT or PRT maps cleanly onto this specific two-bit pairing from either call site alone, and — because this byte is confirmed reused for at least four behaviorally unrelated purposes across its five known bits — cross-referencing it against this project's *trait*-bit registries (as worked for item 3 above) is not the right technique here: `DAT_1128_5a16` is a per-race **runtime/scratch state byte**, not a race-creation trait-selection field, so it has no LRT/PRT "identity" to recover in the first place. **Object-proximity search utility, confirmed by inspection of the exported client and cross-referenced against this document's scanning/detection formulas above.** A separate, generic "find the nearest object to a point" routine is used throughout the client for UI object-picking (e.g., clicking near a planet or fleet on the map, or a "find planet/fleet by name" style lookup) rather than for the turn-generation scan-detection test itself (that test — `distance² <= scanRange²` — is already documented above as its own, separately-verified mechanism). **Two distinct point-distance helpers, resolved by inspection of the exported client — this project's previously-flagged "line-segment/point proximity geometry helper of unconfirmed destination" is now identified and corrected.** What had been carried as one open item is actually two separate routines:

- A **plain squared-distance-between-two-points** utility (no line/segment math at all) is called pervasively throughout the movement and targeting machinery — dozens of call sites across the client's fleet-processing, targeting, and mine-laying code. Traced call sites show it used, among other things, to test whether a fleet has closed to within a fixed snap-to-arrival distance of its next waypoint, and to rank candidate objects by distance when picking a nearest target among several. - A **genuine route-leg-vs-circular-zone proximity routine** is the real match for a "line-segment and point" helper. In effect: "does this travel leg pass within radius of this circular zone, and across what portion of the leg does it stay inside." **The fleet lifecycle cluster, confirmed by inspection of the exported client — a previously-unaccounted-for group of seven functions covering fleet creation, deletion, splitting, and merging, plus the underlying same-location "stack" grouping structure they all depend on.** This segment owns the complete life cycle of a fleet record in the client's in-memory fleet table (the same table walked throughout this document and `client-interface.md`), not merely the integrity/bookkeeping pass described above:

- **Same-location stack grouping.** A dedicated pass rebuilds, from scratch each time it runs, a per-fleet "next fleet at this same map position" linked-list field: it clears every fleet's link field, then walks the fleet table sorted by position (an insertion-sort into a working buffer of up to 62 entries, flushed via a binary-search merge against the remaining table once the buffer fills), chaining together every run of fleets that share exact map coordinates. A small dedicated comparator pair (by-record and by-key) backs both the insertion sort and the binary-search merge. This chain is what several other routines — including the resource aggregator (Group A-adjacent "h" finding above) and the object-identification text builder's "+" (multiple ships/fleets here) suffix — walk to treat co-located fleets as a single stack, and is plausibly also what backs segment 6's independently-documented "cycle co-located objects" click behavior on the map.
- **Fleet creation.** Given an owning race and a design index, a routine allocates a new fleet-table slot (growing the table and re-sorting the insertion point to keep per-race fleet numbers contiguous, filling any numbering gap left by a previous deletion before appending), zeroes the new record, stamps its packed owner/number identifier and design reference, copies a starting map position (from the design's home-planet position when not otherwise specified), allocates its (initially empty) cargo-manifest trailing buffer, and increments a per-race "designs in use" refcount table keyed by design index.
- **Fleet deletion** is the exact inverse: it locates the fleet-table entry, frees its cargo-manifest and name buffers, decrements the same per-race "designs in use" refcount, clears any message-store references that targeted it, compacts the table (shifting later entries down), and — when the deleted fleet was the currently-selected object — re-resolves the UI's current selection to a neighboring table entry or clears it, matching this document's and `client-interface.md`'s working-copy selection bookkeeping.
- **Splitting a fleet** (one ship/unit peeled into its own fleet) is implemented as: create a new fleet via the creation routine above, then copy across the source's cloak state, colonist count, and a resized copy of its cargo-manifest buffer. A second, outer routine implements "Split All" by repeatedly invoking the single-split routine — once per excess unit in every per-cargo/design slot of the source fleet — refreshing the map picker and committing the working-copy record after each split.
- **Merging fleets** takes a target fleet and a caller-supplied list of candidate fleet IDs (the same-location stack described above, in its traced call pattern): candidates owned by the same race as the target have their cargo and fuel totals folded into the target's own fields; candidates of a *different* design instead have five aggregate fields added into a separate part of the target record, after which the now-absorbed candidate fleet is removed via the deletion routine above. A per-wormhole/general "recompute this fleet's working copy" refresh follows.

None of these seven functions were previously accounted for in this segment's tracked findings; together they are the concrete implementation behind the game's user-facing Split Fleet, Split All, and Merge Fleets commands, plus the internal same-location stacking those commands (and several others) depend on.

**Waypoint-task feasibility and partial-fulfillment, confirmed by inspection of the exported client.** Executing a fleet's arrived-at-waypoint task (the same processing this document's §5 route/task mechanism covers) runs a per-ship-design feasibility check before applying the task in full: for each design present in the fleet, a dedicated routine compares a requested amount against that design's freight-relevant capacity fields (its own and, for a two-sided task, a counterpart design's fields), each independently scaled by a fixed polynomial approximation consistent with a distance/range falloff rather than a flat capacity check. A request that clears every design's capacity check outright proceeds at 100%; otherwise the routine returns an explicit infeasible/no-fit outcome, or a reduced percentage of the originally requested amount. The caller then applies that percentage per design slot, and where a design's capacity is under strain, applies an additional **per-unit stochastic loss pass**: for each individual unit being processed for that design, one RNG draw decides survival against a probability derived from the design's own per-slot data and the race's own trait-table lookup (the same generic race-field accessor already confirmed for the mining engine — see `population-growth.md` §5), consistent with the same "RNG-gated stochastic outcome instead of a flat formula" house style already documented elsewhere in the turn engine. Total losses across all designs are then reported to the fleet's owner via the report/message-record store (see `client-ui-dialog-catalog.md`'s "Messages" section) using one of four graduated severity messages selected by comparing total losses against one-quarter and one-half of the amount originally at risk (light / moderate / heavy), plus a distinct fifth message when every design's contribution is fully consumed and none of the amount survives.

**Trigger condition, resolved by inspection of the exported client's single caller of this feasibility routine.** This routine has exactly one call site, deep inside the client's generic per-fleet, per-turn waypoint-task-execution loop (itself called exactly once per turn, as the fleet **movement** pass, step 16 of the reconciled order in `turn-generation-engine.md` §1, before the production pass; the earlier placement in an "economic/production turn pass" came from the old coarse phase list, and the four-mode task dispatcher `FUN_10b0_3f3a` is a different function run twice before and twice after movement). That loop explicitly skips any fleet whose current task carries one of two specific task-type codes — handled by other, separately-documented code elsewhere (one of them by the "Set Waypoint to `<amount>`" conditional cargo routine already confirmed above) — and, for every other task type, requires the task's target (resolved through the same planet-lookup table used throughout the client) to belong to either the fleet's own race or a race flagged as allied in the per-race relationship table, aborting with a distinct message otherwise. **This ally-or-own-race requirement rules out an invasion/ground-combat reading** (invasion specifically targets a hostile, non-allied owner) and instead points toward a mechanic that only ever acts against a friendly or neutral target. Two further details corroborate this: (1) the fleet-to-target *distance* is explicitly computed (via the same coordinate-delta/magnitude helper used elsewhere) and fed into the feasibility check alongside each design's capacity value, confirming the "distance/range falloff" characterization above is a genuine spatial effect, not a flat capacity test; and (2) immediately before the feasibility-and-loss pass, a separate, unconditionally-executed block — gated on the fleet's race lacking one specific racial-trait value already used pervasively elsewhere in this client to gate other per-race exceptions — empties *all* of the fleet's mineral **and** colonist cargo onto the target planet outright, posting one of three fixed messages depending on which of (minerals moved, colonists moved) were nonzero for that turn. Taken together — an ally/own-race-only target, a distance-scaled capacity check, and a forced full cargo offload for every race except one specific trait-gated exception — this combination is the best match, among the mechanics known to exist in *Stars!*, for the client's implementation of the already-documented **Stargate cargo/colonist transit restriction** (§5 "Stargates" above): ordinary races must fully unload mineral and colonist cargo before using a gate (the forced-dump branch), while the one trait-excluded race is instead allowed to carry cargo through a gate and is subject to a distance/capacity-scaled per-design feasibility check whose overflow is resolved through the graduated stochastic casualty pass described above — consistent with this document's existing (previously code-unconfirmed) claim that "Interstellar Traveler races are documented as having a reduced... chance of losing overgated ships to the void, while suffering the same damage percentages as anyone else."

**The excluded race trait is now confirmed to be Interstellar Traveler, by index cross-reference rather than string/label data.** The gate is a single comparison — `stars.exe.export.c` line 73792, inside this feasibility routine's one caller (`FUN_10b0_1f8c`, beginning at line 73289) — reading `iVar8 = FUN_10e0_222c(<per-race record pointer>, 0xe)` and testing `iVar8 != 7`; when true (every PRT except index 7), the forced full-cargo-dump block runs. `FUN_10e0_222c` (defined at line 93561) is a trivial generic byte-field reader, `return *(char *)(param_1 + param_2 + 0x3e)`, called well over 100 times throughout the client with the same field selector `0xe` and, at other call sites, literally compared against 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, and 9 — i.e. it reads a race's Primary Racial Trait index. `ship-design-and-components.md` §5 independently reconstructs, from this same client's per-subtype PRT-gating checks, the exact ordering **Hyper Expansion=0, War Monger=2, Claim Adjuster=3, Inner Strength=4, Space Demolition=5, Packet Physics=6, Interstellar Traveller=7, Alternate Reality=8**, and `race-traits.md`'s live-client observation pass independently confirms **PRT index 9 = Jack Of All Trades**. Index 7 in that convention is Interstellar Traveler, with no ambiguity or conflict between the two documents' reconstructions. This is a code-level index cross-reference, not string/label data, but it is definitive given the clean, corroborated match: **the excluded race trait gating this mechanic is confirmed to be Interstellar Traveler.** The exact real-world identity of the remaining (non-skipped) task-type codes reaching this same generic loop was not individually enumerated.

**`FUN_10b0_1f8c`'s own caller is now identified, and the "called exactly once per turn" framing above is confirmed rather than assumed.** `FUN_10b0_1f8c` has exactly one call site, inside `FUN_10b0_0000` — a segment-23 routine previously mischaracterized in `new-game-setup.md`/`code-coverage-report.md` as a small "new-game start commit" step, now confirmed (see `turn-generation-engine.md`'s new §10) to be the client's actual master turn-generation routine, called from the manual "Generate Turn" command and the batch 10/100/1000-turn commands alike, and reused verbatim to produce a new game's first turn. **A further, previously-unattributed segment-23 routine (`FUN_10b0_312a`) implements the concrete Stargate jump/"Overgating" outcome computation** referenced only abstractly above: given a fleet and a destination gate, it derives safe-range and safe-mass limits from both gates' capability ratings and the fleet's own mass, then selects among several graduated outcomes (safe arrival, a partial-loss/damage outcome, or fleet-destroying failure) reported through distinct messages — consistent with the publicly-documented "Guts of Overgating" mechanic already cited below, though the exact numeric thresholds were not fully re-derived from the decompiled arithmetic this pass. **Re-read this session (`stars.exe.export.c:74080`-`74701`) specifically hunting for the literal threshold constants — a partial result: the function's architecture is now clearer, but the percentage constants themselves remain elusive.** `FUN_10b0_312a` is a single dual-purpose hazard-transit routine, not two separate functions: its third parameter (`param_3`) is `NULL` for the ordinary-movement/minefield call site already fully documented above, and a pointer to the *destination gate's own record* for the Stargate/overgating call site (and, per the paragraph above, for the mass-packet-blast call site). This is offered as a substantive partial result — the mechanism connecting Stargate overgating to minefield-style tiered damage is now confirmed and cited precisely — rather than the full numeric re-derivation the open item asked for.

**Segment 23's largest function, resolved: per-fleet Transport/Scrap/Merge/Lay-Mines/Stargate-jump task execution, confirmed by inspection of the exported client.** `turn-generation-engine.md` §10 previously flagged a ~1,600-line function in segment 23 (`FUN_10b0_3f3a`) — by a wide margin the largest function in that segment — as this project's single largest remaining uncharacterized routine, describing it only as "a per-fleet task-dispatch pass." A full read resolves it. It is a second per-fleet, per-turn task dispatcher, structurally parallel to (and invoked separately from) `FUN_10b0_1f8c`'s feasibility-gated loop documented immediately above: it walks every fleet in the game once per call, reads each fleet's current-waypoint task from a packed field (a low nibble carrying the task-type code, a high nibble carrying an auxiliary target-reference kind that the Transport handler below also consults for its own sub-dispatch), and branches to one of several task-specific handlers. It takes a mode parameter with at least four distinct values across its known call sites, and several handlers behave differently depending on that mode — consistent with a deliberate multi-pass resolution order within the same turn (so, for example, a fleet merging into a second fleet that itself has a pending task resolves correctly regardless of processing order), matching this project's original "four internal modes" description almost exactly. The task handlers resolved this pass:

- **Transport** (the large majority of the function's length, roughly 780 of its ~1,600 lines). For each of five cargo slots — the three mineral types, colonists, and fuel, the same slots and ordering already documented in §4 above — the handler reads a packed per-slot instruction code plus a 12-bit amount, and dispatches through a per-slot instruction switch covering (at least) Load All, Unload All, Load/Unload a fixed Amount, Set Amount To (this document's already-documented §4 "Set Waypoint to `<amount>`" conditional form), Fill to a Percentage, and a habitability-driven "Load Optimal" variant that calls directly into segment 11's habitability/capacity routines (`FUN_1050_29b8`, `FUN_1050_3cee`) to size the load against the destination planet's population capacity rather than a flat amount. Transfers move cargo between the fleet and either a planet or another fleet sharing its map position — searching among same-location candidate fleets when the task's target reference is a fleet rather than a fixed planet ID — and every outcome (amount moved, blocked by relations, insufficient capacity, nothing to move) is logged through the message-record store via one of several dozen distinct message codes. This confirms full Transport-task execution — not just the conditional "Set Waypoint to" sub-case already documented in §4 — lives here, not in `FUN_10b0_1f8c`'s loop; it is consistent with, and plausibly explains, that loop's own documented behavior of skipping any fleet "whose current task carries one of two specific task-type codes, handled by other, separately-documented code elsewhere" (see the "Trigger condition" note above) — Transport is (at least) one of those two skipped codes.
- **Scrap Fleet.** Entered when the task code takes one particular value, or (in one specific processing mode only) a second value gated behind an additional racial-trait check — whether these represent one task with a conditional variant or two genuinely different task codes sharing this handler was not conclusively resolved. It recovers a fraction of the fleet's installed components' mineral value into the stockpile of the planet or starbase it currently occupies (or loses the value entirely if not at a planet), at a rate that varies with location and racial trait — recovery divisors of 3/4/5/10/20 correspond to roughly 33%/25%/20%/10%/5% of value recovered, with the higher recovery rates applying when scrapping at a friendly starbase — then flags the fleet destroyed, using the same "removed from play" bit this function's own opening per-fleet check tests on every fleet. This confirms Scrap Fleet fleets are fully retired the same turn they scrap, not merely marked and cleaned up later.

  **Settled this session, by re-reading the handler's entry gate directly: it is one task code, not two.** A full search of `FUN_10b0_3f3a`'s body for every comparison of the task-nibble variable (`uVar34`) against the literal `5` finds exactly one site (`stars.exe.export.c:75484`): `if (((uVar34 == 5) && (param_1 == 1)) || (uVar34 == 2)) { ... }`. This is the *only* place task-nibble 5 is checked anywhere in the function — there is no second, independent entry point for a distinct "second Scrap Fleet task code." What this condition actually shows is that task-nibble 5 (Scrap Fleet) and task-nibble 2 (Colonize) share one combined entry block, because their setup logic overlaps substantially (both need to resolve and validate a target planet before proceeding) — and, within that shared block, Scrap Fleet's own branch only ever executes during processing mode `1` (`param_1 == 1`) of the dispatcher's four passes; in the other three modes, task-nibble 5 simply does not match either half of the `||` and the fleet falls through unprocessed for that pass. The previously-flagged "apparent second mechanic" is, as this document already establishes, Ultimate Recycling's own alternate branch *within* this same single code path — concretely, `stars.exe.export.c:75559`-`75563`, `if (((local_66 & 0xf) == 5) && (local_c2 != 0xffff)) { iVar22 = FUN_10e0_226e(...,5); ... }`, a race-trait check reached only after task-nibble 5 has already matched. **This also independently confirms, via a second and unrelated route, that selector `5` of `FUN_10e0_226e` is Ultimate Recycling** — matching `research-tech-tree.md`'s existing code-level identification of bit `5` as Ultimate Recycling on the adjacent `0x4e`-offset trait word, and directly explaining why this branch's alternate value-recovery path is gated on it. **Conclusion: Scrap Fleet is a single task code (nibble 5), executed only during processing mode 1 of the four-mode dispatcher; there is no second, distinct task code for it.**
- **Merge With Fleet.** The turn-generation-time execution of a *queued* Merge order — distinct from the immediate "Merge Fleets" UI command already documented above, which executes instantly rather than waiting for a turn to generate. Looks up the target fleet by ID at the same map position, validates it belongs to the same race and is not itself already flagged destroyed, folds the source fleet into it, and tears down the source fleet's own map/icon record.
- **Lay Mine Field.** Gated behind a specific racial-trait check (via the same generic per-race trait-index accessor used pervasively elsewhere in the client) and active in only one specific processing mode. Computes the fleet's total mine-laying output per mine-field type via `FUN_1080_1aea` — this is the per-component mine-laying-rate formula already documented in `turn-generation-engine.md` §3 — then searches the galaxy's minefield table for an existing field of the same owner and type within range to add the new mine units to (recentring the field's position and growing its radius as a strength-weighted average of old and new), or creates a brand-new field when no existing one qualifies. This is the previously-missing other half of the mine-laying mechanic: §3 already documented the *rate* formula, but not where the resulting mine units actually get applied to a minefield.
- **A Stargate-jump completion handler.** Resolves the fleet's target object, and — when it is a friendly planet with an operational Stargate — calls directly into segment 17's Stargate machinery (`FUN_1080_13f8`) to execute the jump and logs a gate-arrival message. When infeasible and running in the mode reserved for final/forced processing, it instead calls a second segment-17 entry point (`FUN_1080_17c2`, plausibly deferring or queuing the attempt) and falls into the same completion code the Merge handler above uses. This is a distinct code path from segment 17's own cargo/colonist transit-restriction check already documented above (the "Waypoint-task feasibility" and "Trigger condition" notes): that check gates *whether* a gating fleet may carry cargo through a Stargate, while this handler is the code that actually *performs* the jump once a fleet is cleared to use one.

**Patrol (7), resolved — it lives in a third, previously-uncharacterized location, not either per-fleet task dispatcher.** It is genuinely absent from both `FUN_10b0_1f8c` and `FUN_10b0_3f3a` (confirmed by re-checking both functions' full task-nibble cascades), because it isn't implemented as a one-shot waypoint-arrival action at all — it's a **radius-gated automatic redirect**, found instead inside a per-race fleet-processing pass in segment 15 (`FUN_1070_374a`, `stars.exe.export.c:47132` onward — a segment previously tracked in this project only for its unrelated galaxy-generation content, now shown to also own this mechanic). Concretely, for a fleet whose current (or, via the same look-ahead promotion already documented for Lay Mine Field above, next-queued) waypoint task is Patrol:

1. It establishes a search origin — the fleet's stored waypoint position if in deep space with more than one waypoint queued, otherwise the fleet's own current position.
2. It scans every fleet in the game, keeping the closest one that (a) the patrolling race can currently detect (a scan-visibility check) and (b) is confirmed hostile via a relationship-lookup call — i.e. **allied and neutral fleets are never treated as patrol targets, only confirmed enemies**, consistent with how targeting eligibility is checked elsewhere in this project (`diplomacy-relations.md`).
3. It reads a stored **patrol range** value from the waypoint record, converts it to light-years via `(storedValue + 1) × 50`, with one reserved stored value (10) mapped to an effectively unlimited 10,000 ly instead — consistent with a small 0-10 range selector in the UI, with the top of the range meaning "unlimited."
4. If the nearest detected hostile falls within that range, it **inserts a brand-new waypoint at the front of the fleet's queue**, positioned at the hostile's current location, tagged with the Patrol task again (so the mechanic re-evaluates on the fleet's next move rather than being a one-time redirect) and carrying forward the same target-reference field the original Patrol order had.

This is a meaningfully different mechanism from ordinary waypoint travel: rather than a single fixed destination, a patrolling fleet gets a **freshly-generated waypoint aimed at whatever the nearest hostile's position is at the moment this pass runs**, each time it runs — which is worth cross-referencing against this document's earlier "No dynamic interception of a moving fleet" finding (§5 above). That finding remains correct for *ordinary* waypoints (a stored, static x/y with no live re-resolution) — Patrol doesn't contradict it, it's a separate, purpose-built exception that achieves a pursuit-like effect for exactly one task type, by re-generating a fresh static waypoint each time it's evaluated rather than by making ordinary waypoints track a moving reference.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Fuel burn for a loaded Privateer at warp 8 and warp 9 (from the original community derivation)

Setup: a race with Improved Fuel Efficiency (IFE, −15% fuel use) has a Fuel-Mizer-engined Privateer carrying 3 fuel pods (1400 mg total fuel capacity). Empty hull mass is 80 kT; fully cargo-loaded it weighs 330 kT. At warp 8 the Fuel Mizer's documented Fuel Usage Number (FUN) is 235.

```
FuelPerLY = (330 / 200) * (2.35) * 0.85 ≈ 3.3 mg/ly
One-way range on 1400 mg = 1400 / 3.3 ≈ 424 ly
```

Now suppose the same ship makes a round trip at warp 9 instead (FUN = 360 at warp 9), loaded (330 kT) outbound and empty (80 kT) on the return leg, and we want the total one-way-equivalent range the 1400 mg tank supports averaged over the full loaded+unloaded round trip:

```
AverageMassMoved = (330 + 80) / 2 * 2 = 410 kT-equivalent weight moved over the whole distance
FuelPerLY = (410 / 200) * 3.60 * 0.85 ≈ 6.27 mg/ly
RoundTripRange = 1400 / 6.27 ≈ 223 ly (i.e., the ship can go 223 ly out and 223 ly back on one tank)
```

This shows the double lever a heavier load and a higher warp factor both pull on fuel economy — going one warp faster (8→9) and hauling the return trip's weight into the average very nearly doubles the mg/ly cost per light-year versus the one-way loaded-only figure.

Source: [Guts, §4.5.1 "Guts of Fuel Usage" (attributed to Jason Cawley) — starsfaq.com](http://www.starsfaq.com/advfaq/guts2.htm)

### Example 2 — Combining three scanners on one design

A custom scout design mounts two 100-ly scanners and one 60-ly scanner (for illustration; in the real game most hulls only have one scanner slot type, but multi-scanner combining applies whenever more than one is present, e.g., via a built-in hull scanner plus a mounted component):

```
CombinedRange = (100^4 + 100^4 + 60^4)^(1/4)
             = (100,000,000 + 100,000,000 + 12,960,000)^(1/4)
             = (212,960,000)^(1/4)
             ≈ 120.8 ly
```

The combined range (≈121 ly) is only marginally better than either single 100-ly scanner alone, illustrating why stacking multiple scanners on one hull gives sharply diminishing returns compared to just using the single best available scanner.

Source: [Scanner Technology — Stars!wiki (help-file mirror)](https://wiki.starsautohost.org/wikinew/hlp/index_web/148.html)

### Example 3 — Detecting a cloaked scout, and a minefield's independent detection roll

A defending empire has a planetary scanner rated at 150 ly (non-penetrating). An enemy scout arrives 80%-cloaked.

```
EffectiveDetectionRange = 150 ly * (100% - 80%) = 150 * 0.20 = 30 ly
```

The cloaked scout is invisible to that scanner anywhere beyond 30 ly, even though the scanner's rated range is 150 ly. If that same scout instead flies through a Space Demolition race's minefield, a second and independent detection roll applies regardless of distance to any scanner:

```
P(detected by minefield, per year inside it) = 100% - 80% = 20%
```

So over, say, 3 years spent transiting the minefield, the scout's chance of being spotted by the minefield alone (ignoring re-rolls' independence caveats) is noticeably higher than a single year's 20%, even though the planetary scanner never sees it at all outside 30 ly.

Source: [Chapter 9: Intelligence — Stars!wiki](https://web.archive.org/web/20210731001838/https://wiki.starsautohost.org/wiki/Chapter_9:Intelligence), [Stars! Strategy Guide, §PG2-06 "Space Demolition" — GameFAQs](https://web.archive.org/web/20210615103545/https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/pc/198797-stars/faqs/41043)

- ~~**Overgating's exact numeric thresholds not fully re-derived.**~~ **The four table constants are now resolved; the percentage-formula mapping remains open.** `FUN_10b0_312a` is confirmed to be a single dual-purpose hazard-transit routine (its third parameter selects minefield vs. Stargate/mass-packet mode at each branch, rather than the function splitting into two separate halves), and overgating severity is confirmed to be bucketed through the *same* generic per-tier constant table that drives minefield damage. **This session applied the CS-relative-addressing technique to the table offsets themselves** (`0xea6`/`0xe9a`/`0xeb2`/`0xeb8`, none of which resolve to a `DAT_` symbol): they are 16-bit constants embedded directly in segment 23's own code, in a gap between two functions (segment 23 begins at file offset `0x6e280`; the gap runs from a `retf` at `0x6f119` to the next function's prologue at `0x6f142`). The index-0 values, read straight from the executable, are **`0xea6`=100, `0xe9a`=500, `0xeb2`=4, `0xeb8`=3**, with further tiers/types at `0xea8`=125, `0xeaa`=500, `0xeac`=600, `0xe9c`=600, `0xe9e`=2000, `0xea0`=2500, `0xeb4`=6, `0xeb6`=5, `0xeba`=10, `0xebc`=35. Matching these raw values term-for-term against the community `RangeDamagePercent`/`MassDamagePercent`/`A≈68` formulas was not attempted this pass.
